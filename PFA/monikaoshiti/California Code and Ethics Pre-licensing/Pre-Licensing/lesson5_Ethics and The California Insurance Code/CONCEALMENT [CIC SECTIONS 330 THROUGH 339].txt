CONCEALMENT [CIC SECTIONS 330 THROUGH 339]

An applicant for insurance is also obligated to voluntarily disclose any other material fact concerning the exposure to be insured, even if the insurer and its agent do not ask questions regarding it. The failure to disclose material facts is considered to be concealment. In other words, an insured may not withhold any material facts that he should realize the insurer would want to know. Sections 330 – 339 state that concealment is fraudulent in nature and is grounds for voiding the policy. Concealment may be defined as neglecting to communicate that which a party knows and ought to communicate. Most contract law regulations state that even if concealment is intentional or unintentional, an injured party or an insurer is entitled to rescind the contract. This means that parties to a contract must communicate to one another all material facts within their knowledge or any material information that the other has no means to discover. Section 331 states that either intentional or unintentional concealment entitles an injured party to rescission of a contract.
Generally, parties to a contract are not required to communicate the following information:
• Those which the other knows;
• Those which, in the exercise of ordinary care, the other ought to know, and of which the party has no reason to suppose him ignorant;
• Those of which the other waives communication;
• Those which prove or tend to prove the existence of a risk excluded by a warranty, and which are not otherwise material; and
• Those which relate to a risk excepted from insurance, and which are not otherwise material.
With regard to an insurance contract, all intentional and fraudulent omissions by an insured, in order to prove the falsity of a warranty, entitles the insurer to rescind.
For example, if an applicant withholds information about his health status that he should have disclosed, he has probably engaged in concealment.
Each party to a contract of insurance is bound to know:
• All the general causes which are open to his inquiry equally with that of the other, and which may affect either the political or material perils contemplated.
• All the general usages of trade.
The right to information of material facts may be waived, either by the terms of insurance or by neglect to make inquiries as to such facts, where they are distinctly implied in other facts of which information is communicated.
WARRANTY, SECTION 440 THROUGH 449 OF THE CIC;
A warranty is a statement made by the applicant / insured that is guaranteed to be absolutely true. Such a statement is made part of the contract and then becomes a warranty — a promise made by the insured on which coverage is based. If found to be untrue, the insurer may revoke or rescind the contract. A warranty is presumed to be material since it affects the insurer’s decision to accept or reject an applicant’s offer for insurance coverage. A warranty may be expressed or implied and related to the past, present or future. A warranty is “expressed” when a matter relating to an insured is stated as a fact in the policy. When stated in a contract, an intention to do or not to do something that materially affects the risk is a warranty that an act or omission will occur. If a warranty is violated by one party, the other party has the right to rescind. Section 440 through 449 states that an implied warranty is something that is not written but involves a condition that is understood to be true (i.e., that a ship is seaworthy). For example, due to several robberies at a small branch office of a bank, an insurer informs the bank that it will not provide crime coverage unless the bank installs a new alarm system and hires a guard who will always be on duty during banking hours. In order to receive coverage, the bank warranties (i.e., guarantees in writing) that it will comply. This warranty is physically made part of the policy. If the bank is robbed when the guard left the premises to buy lunch, the insurer could deny coverage because the warranty was breached.
MATERIALITY, SECTION 334 OF THE CIC;
The materiality of concealment is the rule used to determine the importance of a misrepresentation. Materiality is to be determined not by the event, but solely by the probable and reasonable influence of the facts upon the party to whom the communication is due, in forming his estimate of the disadvantages of the proposed contract, or in making his inquiries.
